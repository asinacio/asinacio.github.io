I am a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Postdoctoral Fellow in the Department of Physics at the University of Oxford.

I'm an experimental particle physicist interested in understanding the fundamental properties of neutrinos and what they can tell us about the Universe. My research focuses on rare-event searches and precision neutrino measurements, combining detector calibration, instrumentation, and data analysis to improve the sensitivity of large-scale underground experiments. I am a member of the SNO+ collaboration, where I work on topics ranging from solar neutrinos to searches for neutrinoless double beta decay.

What I enjoy most about my research is solving challenging experimental problems, whether that means developing new calibration techniques, designing newanalysis methods, or finding ways to extract tiny signals from large datasets. Outside the lab, I'm passionate about teaching, mentoring, and sharing the excitement of particle physics with wider audiences through outreach and public engagement.

## Background

I grew up in a small village in central Portugal, where I was the kind of child who wanted to know how everything worked. I was fascinated by the natural sciences, but also by mathematics, history, music, and just about anything that sparked my curiosity. Physics appealed to me because of its interdisciplinary nature—it seemed like a field where I could explore ideas that connected many different areas of knowledge.

While I was in high school, I came across a post on a Portuguese science blog telling the story of Ettore Majorana and the mysterious "ghost particle," the neutrino. I understood very little about particle physics at the time, but I was completely captivated. Years later, after studying Physics at the University of Lisbon and completing a PhD in experimental particle physics, I found myself working on neutrino experiments. Looking back, it's remarkable that a story I stumbled upon as a teenager would end up shaping my career.
