Looking for very small signals
that could tell us something very big.

I work on low-energy neutrinos, rare-event searches, and the detectors built to find them.
Much of my work comes down to a deceptively simple problem:
understanding a tiny signal in a very complicated detector.

Solar Neutrinos

Solar neutrinos are produced deep inside the Sun and arrive on Earth carrying information from a place we cannot observe directly.

Measuring them allows us to probe both the processes that power the Sun and the properties of neutrinos themselves. At low energies, however, these measurements are particularly challenging: solar neutrino signals must be disentangled from radioactive backgrounds and other processes that can look remarkably similar in the detector.

My work focuses on extracting these signals from the data, understanding the backgrounds that surround them, and developing analysis strategies to improve the sensitivity of low-energy solar neutrino measurements.

I am currently the convener of the SNO+ solar neutrino analysis group, where I lead a team of researchers working on the experiment's solar neutrino programme.

Beyond precision measurements of the expected solar neutrino flux, I am also interested in using the Sun as a source for searches for unexpected phenomena. These include solar antineutrinos, whose observation could point to new neutrino interactions and physics beyond the Standard Model.

Neutrinoless Double-Beta Decay

This is the kind of experiment where not seeing any neutrinos is the expected result.

Neutrinoless double-beta decay is an exceptionally rare process — if it occurs at all. Its observation would demonstrate that lepton number is not conserved, provide evidence that neutrinos are Majorana particles, and offer a new window onto the neutrino mass scale.

Searching for such a rare decay means understanding everything that could imitate it. Radioactive backgrounds, detector effects, and other physical processes can all produce events in the region where a signal would appear, making their identification and modelling a central part of the search.

Within the SNO+ experiment, I have worked on background studies and modelling, detector calibration, and sensitivity studies for the neutrinoless double-beta decay search.

Liquid Scintillator Detectors

A particle passes through a detector, deposits a little energy, and leaves behind light.

Large liquid scintillator detectors are powerful tools for studying rare events and low-energy neutrino interactions. Their sensitivity depends not only on their size, but on how well we understand the scintillator, the light it produces, and the response of the photodetectors around it.

My interests include detector design and performance, scintillator properties, optical response, event reconstruction, and the practical details that determine whether a detector can actually deliver the physics we want from it.

Detector Calibration & Modelling

A signal is not useful if you do not understand the detector that measured it.

Particle physics measurements depend on understanding how the detector responds to different types of events, how that response changes with position and energy, and how backgrounds appear in the data.

My work includes detector calibration, background modelling, simulation, and performance studies — the less visible parts of an experiment that ultimately determine how confidently we can interpret its results.
