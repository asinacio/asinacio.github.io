Looking for very small signals
that could tell us something very big.

I work on low-energy neutrinos, rare-event searches, and the detectors built to find them. Much of my work comes down to a deceptively simple problem: understanding a tiny signal in a very complicated detector.

02 Featured research
PROJECT / 01

Low-Energy Neutrino Physics

The Sun is sending us a remarkably detailed message. The difficult part is hearing it.

Solar neutrinos provide a way to study both neutrino properties and the processes taking place deep inside the Sun.

What makes the problem interesting experimentally is that the signal we want is surrounded by backgrounds that can look remarkably similar to it. My work focuses on how to separate those contributions and improve the sensitivity of low-energy measurements.

I am also interested in searches for less conventional signals, such as solar antineutrinos, where an unexpected observation could point towards new neutrino interactions or physics beyond the Standard Model.

PROJECT / 02

Neutrinoless Double-Beta Decay

This is the kind of experiment where not seeing anything is the expected result.

Neutrinoless double-beta decay is an exceptionally rare process — if it occurs at all. Its observation would demonstrate that lepton number is not conserved and would provide evidence that the neutrino is a Majorana particle.

The experimental challenge is therefore unusually demanding: a convincing signal needs to survive an enormous amount of scrutiny. Understanding the backgrounds is just as important as understanding what a signal would look like.

Within the SNO+ experiment, I have worked on the study and modelling of backgrounds, detector calibration, and performance studies relevant to the search.

PROJECT / 03

Liquid-Scintillator Detectors

There is something slightly absurd about building a detector the size of a building to catch a handful of particles.

Large liquid-scintillator detectors are powerful tools for studying rare and low-energy neutrino interactions. Their sensitivity depends not only on their size, but on how well we understand the scintillator, the light it produces, and the response of the photodetectors around it.

My interests include detector design and performance, scintillator behaviour, optical response, event reconstruction, and the practical details that determine whether a detector can actually deliver the physics we want from it.

PROJECT / 04

Detector Calibration & Modelling

Before asking what the universe is doing, you have to make sure you understand your detector.

Precision measurements depend on understanding how the detector responds to different types of events, how that response changes with position and energy, and how backgrounds appear in the data.

My work includes detector calibration, background modelling, simulation, and performance studies — the less visible parts of an experiment that ultimately determine how confidently we can interpret its results.

03 Questions

At the centre of my work are a few fairly simple questions that turn out to be experimentally difficult.

01

What does a very small signal look like when almost everything around it is background?

02

How much can low-energy solar neutrinos tell us about the neutrino — and about the inside of the Sun?

03

How do you convince yourself that a rare event is real?

04

How well can we understand a detector when the thing we are trying to measure is almost never there?

05

What might neutrinos be telling us that the Standard Model has not yet explained?

06

How much more can we learn if we can make the detector a little better, the background a little smaller, and the analysis a little sharper?
